## 1. Recurring principles from people who write in public

- **Write for the reader who's deciding whether to keep reading, not for a
  résumé committee.** Standard advice, but Sivers' own page inverts it
  deliberately: "This is just one big page where I can write all about
  myself, without feeling the need for it to be useful." (verified) —
  [About Derek Sivers](https://sive.rs/about), Derek Sivers, page last updated
  2026-05-13. Counter-evidence: Sivers can afford this because his readers
  already trust him; a page meant to convert a first-time visitor in 30
  seconds can't skip utility the way his does.
- **Credentials belong in narrative, not a list.** Will Larson's about opens
  "Hi folks, I'm a software engineering leader, technology writer, and
  currently the CTO at Imprint," then folds Stripe/Uber/Carta/Calm and four
  books into prose rather than a bullet CV. (verified) —
  [About — Will Larson, Irrational Exuberance](https://lethain.com/about/).
- **First person, always.** Every practitioner page found (Sivers, Willison,
  Larson, McKenzie) is first person; none uses "he" of themselves. (inferred
  from the four pages read directly — no single source states this as a
  rule, but it's the consistent pattern across all of them.)
- **State why you write, once, plainly — don't perform passion.** Larson and
  McKenzie both state their focus as a fact ("systems thinking applied to
  businesses") rather than an enthusiasm claim. (inferred from reading both
  pages; neither uses "passionate.")
- **End with a next step, not a summary.** Larson's page ends with a link:
  "Want help with something? Here are ways I'm available to help!" —
  advisory work stated once, at the close, not threaded through the bio.
  (verified) — [lethain.com/about](https://lethain.com/about/).
- **Disclosure over polish, when you have conflicts to disclose.** Simon
  Willison's about page devotes a full section to sponsorships, board seats
  and AI-company preview access rather than smoothing them out. (verified) —
  [About Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/about/).
- **Short.** McKenzie's page is roughly 350 words and still does bio,
  positioning and contact. (verified, word count from direct read) —
  [About patio11 — Kalzumeus](https://www.kalzumeus.com/about/).

Only one non-practitioner source used, because it names the failure modes
directly with a quote: see section 3.

## 2. About pages that do this well

**Derek Sivers — [sive.rs/about](https://sive.rs/about).** Opens "This is
just one big page where I can write all about myself, without feeling the
need for it to be useful." Structurally: chronological timeline first, then
thematic reflections on work and values; credentials (Berklee, CD Baby, TED,
books) surface inside the story, never listed. Ends "No comments here. That
would be too weird. This is too personal. Just email me" — a private-contact
close, not a form.

**Will Larson — [lethain.com/about](https://lethain.com/about/).** Opens with
current role stated flat, then career history in prose, then published
books, background, contact. Ends with a link to his advisory offer and a
list of popular posts. Structurally short: role → history → work → how to
reach him → what to read next. The advisory line is one sentence, placed
last, not the frame for the whole page.

**Patrick McKenzie — [kalzumeus.com/about](https://www.kalzumeus.com/about/).**
Opens "My name is Patrick McKenzie. I'm a recovering Japanese salaryman who
ran a succession of small software companies." Structured as anticipated
questions ("Who *are* you, anyway?"), self-positions with one dry line —
"a better engineer than almost all marketers and a better marketer than
almost all engineers" — then lists current projects (newsletter, podcast) at
the end. About 350 words. Ends on contact details and a copyright line, no
sales pitch.

**Simon Willison — [simonwillison.net/about](https://simonwillison.net/about/).**
Opens with what he does now, not who he's been: "creator of Datasette... now
building open source tools for data journalism." Structured: bio →
newsletter/feed links → a long disclosures section → site's own tech stack.
Ends on infrastructure trivia and a decade-by-decade archive — an
unusually technical, unpolished close for someone widely read.

Rachel Andrew ([rachelandrew.co.uk/about](https://rachelandrew.co.uk/about/))
and Camille Fournier (camilletalk.com) were located but not fetched directly —
search results gave career facts, not page structure or quotable lines, so
they aren't included as verified examples here. Flagging so planning can
decide whether they're worth a direct read.

## 3. Failure modes

- **Third-person bio.** No practitioner page in this set uses it; Nic's own
  existing about.html already names and rejects it: "I'm not going to write a
  third-person bio. That would be weird." (verified, from repo file)
- **CV/resume dump.** Named directly: "you have no idea how to make your
  About page sound less like a resume and more like a story." (verified) —
  [The Not-About-You About Page Formula](https://betweenthelinescopy.com/blog/not-about-you-page-formula/),
  Sara, betweenthelinescopy.com, 16 May 2022.
- **"Me, me, me" framing that never reaches the reader.** Same source:
  pages that read as "learn all about me, me, me!" rather than conversational.
  Counter-evidence: this is a marketing-copywriting source, not a
  practitioner; the advice tips toward sales copy. Used only for the named
  failure, not the fix.
- **The "I help X do Y" template and "passionate about."** No single source
  quoted these verbatim in the material gathered; general copywriting
  searches returned formula-selling sites (PASTOR, ARTIST) recommending
  exactly this kind of template, which is itself evidence the pattern is an
  industry default worth avoiding for a peer-to-peer voice. (inferred, not a
  direct quote against it)
- **Ending with nothing to do.** No direct source states this as a named
  failure; inferred from the contrast between practitioner pages (Larson,
  McKenzie, Sivers) which all end on an action — email, link, or explicit
  "here's how to reach me" — and generic templates, which end on summary
  copy. (inferred)

## 4. Writer-and-buyer pages (writing + consulting from one site)

Best two direct examples for this:

- **Will Larson** keeps the advisory pitch to one line, placed after the
  writing bio, not before it: "Want help with something? Here are ways I'm
  available to help!" links out rather than pitching in place. The about
  page itself stays a writer's page; the sales page is a separate, linked
  destination. (verified)
- **Patrick McKenzie** folds the positioning into a single self-aware line —
  "a better engineer than almost all marketers and a better marketer than
  almost all engineers" — which does double duty: it's funny enough to read
  as personality, and it's also, functionally, a pitch. He doesn't separate
  "about" from "hire me"; he compresses the pitch into voice. (verified)

Common thread across both (inferred, not stated by either as a rule): the
buyer-facing line is short, appears once, and is placed after the writing
identity is established — never before it, and never repeated. Neither page
uses second-person sales language ("I help you...") anywhere in the bio
itself.

No source directly addresses "how to keep both audiences served without
becoming a sales page" as a stated principle — this section is built from
reading the two pages side by side, not from a single citable claim.
