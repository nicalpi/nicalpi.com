## Opening B

In January 2026 I froze my team's hiring plan. I wanted to understand what AI was going to do to an eight-person development team before I added people to it, and I didn't have a prediction. Seven months later the whole team works through an AI workflow, the gain turned out to be in planning and ticket quality rather than speed, and hiring has reopened. That's roughly how I work: change one thing, watch what happens, then decide.

## What I've learned the hard way

The best CTOs I've met aren't the busiest ones. They've designed systems, teams and lives that don't depend on heroics, so a bad week stays a bad week. Boring technology wins, and complexity is a choice that someone always pays for later. A meeting nobody has examined in a year is debt like any other, and burnout is information about the system someone was working in, not about the person.

## Why field notes

I write here because too much leadership advice performs a certainty the author doesn't have. I stopped publishing for a few years after the sale and started again [… when, and what got you writing again?], and what comes out now are field notes: what I tried with my own team, and what happened. They cover leading teams, using AI without losing ownership of what gets built, and getting useful work done without adding another system to your day. Most of them come back to the same thing: AI made output cheap, and good judgement is now the advantage.

If you want to know whether they're worth your time, start with [the one on measuring AI by speed](/blog/ai-measuring-wrong-thing) and check it against what you're seeing in your own team.
