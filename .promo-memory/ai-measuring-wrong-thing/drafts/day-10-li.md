Seven months of AI on an eight-person team, and the biggest change was who does which part of the thinking.

Scoping is my job. I tell the workflow which sensor we need to integrate, hand it the documentation and what we know, and it turns that into a multi-card epic with user stories and QA acceptance criteria for every sub-ticket.

Delivery planning is the developer's job. The workflow then helps them turn that scope into a locked-in delivery plan, ticket by ticket. That's exactly where it changed how they behave, because it forces the planning conversation whether or not the developer would have had it on their own.

The fair pushback is that this is what senior engineers have always done, and I'm just calling it an AI workflow. Yes. That's right. And that's the whole point. Being able to understand a problem and translate it into a clear plan was a very desirable skill in a developer before. It's now mandatory if they want to be productive with AI.

The part I hold on to: every developer, including the ones who resisted because they liked to "feel in control", said after a few months that they feel they're doing a better job with it, and that they haven't lost ownership of what gets built.
