## 1. Content atomization / waterfall frameworks

**Inferred**: marketing sources describe "content atomization" as breaking one
core asset into platform-specific pieces, roughly 2-3 LinkedIn assets, 2-5
Twitter threads, 3-7 Instagram assets, 2-4 short videos per blog post — but no
single rigorous study backs these numbers; they read as agency-blog convention.
Source: [Content Atomization: How to Use One Blog on Platforms — Azura](https://azuramagazine.com/articles/content-atomization-how-to-repurpose-one-blog-across-multiple-platforms),
undated. Quote: "aim for 2–3 LinkedIn assets, 2–5 Twitter threads, 3–7 Instagram
assets, and 2–4 short-form videos from each blog."

**Counter-evidence**: when I fetched a second atomization source directly
([The Content Atomization Playbook, Bluetext, Eddie Bridgewater, 9 Sep 2025](https://bluetext.com/blog/the-content-atomization-playbook-one-idea-dozens-of-deliverables/)),
it gave no numeric breakdown at all — just qualitative examples ("Three blog
posts... An infographic... A LinkedIn carousel..."). The precise-sounding counts
in other sources don't survive a direct read of a primary article; they appear
to be search-engine-summary artifacts, not verified data.

**Verified**: the hub-and-spoke / pillar model itself is a real, widely-used
framing, not just an SEO-blog invention — it matches the GaryVee model below
independently. Source: [Digital Marketing Institute glossary: Content Waterfall](https://digitalmarketinginstitute.com/resources/glossary/content-waterfall-gls),
undated. Quote: "A content waterfall is a systematic approach in content
marketing that takes a core piece of content (often called 'pillar content')
and reuses it across different channels."

**Inferred, weak**: "94% of marketers say repurposing is the most effective
strategy for maximizing ROI" is repeated across many 2025-2026 marketing-stat
roundup sites, attributed inconsistently — some to Content Marketing Institute,
others to Referral Rock or Semrush. I could not confirm the original survey.
Treat this number as unverified when citing.

**Counter-evidence on repurposing difficulty**: Content Marketing Institute's
own 2025 B2B benchmark research reports the opposite emphasis — repurposing is
listed as an active *challenge*, not a solved playbook, for a meaningful share
of marketers. Source: [B2B Content Marketing: 2025 Benchmarks & Trends, Content Marketing Institute](https://contentmarketinginstitute.com/b2b-research/b2b-content-marketing-trends-research-2025).
Cited figure in secondary summary: "content repurposing is a challenge for 37%
of marketers according to their 2025 B2B Content Marketing Benchmarks study."
(I did not independently verify this % inside the CMI report itself — flagging
as inferred from a secondary summary.)

## 2. Promotion calendar templates (1-2 week window)

**Verified**: a documented 7-day launch calendar exists with a specific
day-by-day structure.
Source: [How to Plan a Launch Week on Social Media, Sol Ericson, Smart Post Studio, 27 Jul 2026](https://smartpoststudio.com/blog/social-media-launch-week-plan).
Exact breakdown, quoted:
- Days 1-2, Tease: "Lead with the problem, crack the door, hint at 'next week'"
- Day 3, Build: "Show more, give a reason to show up on launch day"
- Day 4, Launch: "Best asset, clear transformation, link everywhere, reply for an hour"
- Day 5, Prove: "An early win, screenshot, or first review — proof beats promises"
- Day 6, Answer: "Tackle the top objection or FAQ head-on"
- Day 7, Close: "Last call, recap the offer, one final clear ask"

Also states: "Schedule all content before launch week begins so you can focus
on engagement rather than daily posting logistics."

**Counter-evidence**: this is a single agency's template for a product launch,
not blog-post promotion specifically, and not derived from a controlled study.
No comparably rigorous "2-week blog promotion calendar" source turned up in
this search; treat any day-by-day calendar as a practitioner heuristic, not
data.

## 3. GaryVee content model

**Verified**: the model is real and documented directly by Vaynerchuk's own
site, structured as pillar content -> micro content -> platform distribution
-> community feedback -> more micro content.
Source: [The GaryVee Content Strategy, garyvaynerchuk.com](https://garyvaynerchuk.com/the-garyvee-content-strategy-how-to-grow-and-distribute-your-brands-social-media-content/), undated.
Quote: "my team is able to repurpose that one piece of content into dozens of
smaller pieces of content, contextual to the platforms."
Scale example cited in the same piece: one keynote converted into "30+ pieces
of content, generating over 35 million total views across all platforms."

**Verified**: the underlying philosophy is "Document, Don't Create" — the
pillar is documentation of something already happening (a vlog, a Q&A), not a
manufactured asset. Source: same page, and corroborated by [Gary Vee Content Model: 3 Keys, Gist](https://getgist.com/gary-vee-content-model/), undated.

**Counter-evidence**: this model was built for a video-first personal brand
with a full production team clipping daily footage. It assumes a pillar format
(video) that atomizes cheaply into clips; a written blog post does not
atomize the same way (no natural "highlight reel"), so the piece-count claims
(30+ pieces per keynote) may not transfer to text-first creators.

## 4. Engagement-first vs broadcast ratio

**Inferred**: several 2025-2026 LinkedIn-algorithm explainer sites converge on
an 80/20 value-to-promotion ratio, and a more granular "3/2/1" variant (3 value
posts, 2 personal/story posts, 1 promotional post). These are practitioner
heuristics, not peer-reviewed, and I found no primary platform documentation
(LinkedIn itself does not publish this ratio).
Source: [How the LinkedIn Algorithm Works in 2025, AuthoredUp](https://authoredup.com/blog/linkedin-algorithm), undated, citing Richard van der Blom's Algorithm Insights research.
Quote: "Posts that send readers off LinkedIn get less initial distribution."

**Verified detail with a real named research source** (Richard van der Blom,
an independent LinkedIn-algorithm researcher who runs annual studies):
link-count effect is nonlinear — "1 link: lowest reach... 4+ links: 3-5x
higher median reach than single-link posts." Also: "1 save gives your post 5x
more reach than 1 like," and saves beat "meaningful comments" roughly 2x on
reach impact.

**Counter-evidence**: the same source's own distribution-mix data undercuts
the "post value, skip links" advice for anyone trying to drive blog traffic —
even top-performing creator content only gets ~31% of total feed distribution
share, meaning the promotional constraint is structural, not a matter of
better copywriting. There's also no independent audit of van der Blom's
methodology available in the sources I found; his numbers are widely quoted
but self-published.

## 5. Cross-platform sequencing

**Inferred**: the consensus across practitioner sources is "adapt, don't
duplicate" and stagger except for time-bound announcements.
Source: [Is cross-posting the same content on Twitter and LinkedIn effective?, Circleboom](https://circleboom.com/blog/is-cross-posting-the-same-content-on-twitter-and-linkedin-effective), undated.
Quote: "Cross posting the same content on Twitter and LinkedIn is effective
when you adapt and weak when you duplicate."

**Inferred**: frequency norms differ sharply by platform, which is the
practical reason to stagger rather than post simultaneously — cited posting
cadence: "Twitter – post 5 times a day to get the most retweets... LinkedIn –
post once a day on weekdays," per the same secondary roundup (original studies
not independently verified here).

**Counter-evidence**: no source directly tested simultaneous vs. staggered
posting for the same message with a controlled reach comparison; all guidance
found is inference from platform norms, not an experiment.

## 6. What drives click-through vs. on-platform engagement

**Verified concept, not a click-rate stat**: curiosity gap is grounded in
established psychology research (Loewenstein's Information Gap Theory), cited
secondhand from Harvard Business Review coverage.
Source: [The Curiosity Gap, Enchanting Marketing](https://www.enchantingmarketing.com/curiosity-gap/), undated.
Quote: "curiosity triggers the brain's reward system much like a craving for
food," referencing HBR research on curiosity (I did not independently pull the
HBR article; this is a secondhand citation).

**Inferred**: the specific mechanism recommended for combining value and
click-through is "deliver on the hook's promise inside the platform post, then
use the gap for the parts only the blog post can answer" — paraphrased across
multiple hook-writing guides, not a single verified study, e.g.
[Social Media Hooks: The Complete Guide, Admove](https://www.admove.ai/blog/social-media-hooks-guide), undated.

**Counter-evidence**: LinkedIn's own algorithm penalty for outbound links
(section 4 above) means a strong curiosity-gap hook can still underperform on
reach purely because it contains a link — click-through psychology and
platform distribution mechanics pull in opposite directions.

## 7. Visual asset formats

**Verified, named source with methodology disclosed**: LinkedIn carousels
outperform text posts by a stated margin, from a large-sample study.
Source: [11 LinkedIn Carousel Examples, Buffer, 25 May 2026](https://buffer.com/resources/linkedin-carousel-examples/), citing Buffer's State of Social Media Engagement Report, analysis of "52+ million posts across 200,000+ accounts."
Quote: "LinkedIn carousels have the highest engagement rate on the platform,
performing 585% better than text posts."

**Inferred**: "designed to be screenshotted, not just swiped" is offered as
the design principle behind why carousels outperform, across several carousel
how-to guides — not independently tested, just repeated practitioner advice.

**Counter-evidence**: the 585% figure is an aggregate across all LinkedIn
carousel posts vs. all text posts — it says nothing about carousels made *from
a blog post specifically* vs. a well-written native text post on the same
topic. Applying an aggregate engagement-rate comparison to a single use case
(promoting one blog post) is a category error the source itself doesn't make,
but secondary blog coverage of it often implies.

## Overall caution

Most sources in this space are agency/SaaS marketing blogs optimizing for SEO,
not independent research. The two most load-bearing, attributable data points
found are: Richard van der Blom's LinkedIn algorithm research (self-published,
widely cited, not independently audited) and Buffer's State of Social Media
Engagement Report (disclosed sample size, single-platform vendor's own data).
Treat specific percentages as directional, not precise.
